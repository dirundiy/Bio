Cell Energetics

How cells capture, store and spend energy. Enzymes set the speed of every reaction. Chloroplasts turn light into sugar. Mitochondria turn sugar into ATP. Work through the seven topics in order, then test yourself.

The energy loop the whole unit is built on

Big picture

The products of photosynthesis are the reactants of respiration, and the reverse. Energy does not cycle: it enters as light and leaves as heat, which is why life needs a constant input.

Topics

Four ideas that show up everywhere

Shape decides function

An enzyme works only while its active site keeps its shape. Heat, pH, salt and inhibitors all act by changing that shape or blocking it.

Energy only flows one way

Every transfer loses some energy as heat (2nd law). Cells stay organized only with a steady energy input.

Electrons ride carriers

NADH, FADH₂ and NADPH carry high-energy electrons from one stage to the next. Follow the electrons and the pathways make sense.

Gradients make ATP

Both organelles pump H⁺ across a membrane, then let it flow back through ATP synthase. This is chemiosmosis, and it shows up on almost every exam.

Topic 3.1

Enzyme Structure

Enzymes are biological catalysts, usually proteins. Their power comes from one small region: the active site.

You should be able to
  • Describe the properties of enzymes.
  • Explain how a change in an enzyme's structure changes its function.

What an enzyme is

Most enzymes are proteins folded into a specific 3-D shape. A few are RNA molecules called ribozymes (the ribosome is one). Enzymes speed up reactions without being used up, so a single enzyme molecule can catalyze the same reaction thousands of times per second. Their names usually end in -ase: lactase breaks down lactose, DNA polymerase builds DNA.

The molecule an enzyme acts on is its substrate. The substrate binds in the active site, a pocket or groove on the enzyme's surface, forming an enzyme–substrate complex. The enzyme converts the substrate into products, releases them, and is ready again.

The catalytic cycle

Diagram

The enzyme leaves the reaction unchanged. In the middle panel the active site tightens around the substrate: that is induced fit.

Why enzymes are specific

The active site is lined with the R groups of particular amino acids. Their shape, charge and polarity match only certain substrates. A positively charged region attracts a negatively charged substrate; a nonpolar pocket holds a nonpolar substrate. Shape and chemistry have to fit.

The older lock-and-key model treated the active site as rigid. The current induced-fit model says the active site changes shape slightly when the substrate binds, gripping it more tightly and putting stress on the bonds that need to break.

Structure → function chain

Primary sequence → folding (secondary, tertiary, sometimes quaternary) → shape of the active site → which substrate fits → what reaction happens.

One amino acid can matter

A mutation that swaps an active-site amino acid (say, nonpolar to charged) can stop substrate binding. A swap far from the active site can still matter if it changes how the whole protein folds.

Exam move

When a question says an enzyme "changed," write the full chain: change in structure → change in active-site shape or charge → substrate binds less well → lower reaction rate. Graders look for every link.

Topic 3.2

Enzyme Catalysis

Enzymes don't add energy to a reaction. They lower the hill the reactants have to climb.

You should be able to
  • Explain how enzymes affect the rate of biological reactions.
  • Read and calculate reaction rates from data.

Activation energy

Even reactions that release energy need a push to start. Bonds in the reactants must be strained or broken before new bonds form. That starting energy is the activation energy (Eₐ). At body temperature, few molecules have enough energy to get over a high Eₐ barrier, so the reaction is slow.

Enzymes lower Eₐ. More molecules can get over the smaller barrier, so the reaction runs much faster at the same temperature.

Energy diagram

Interactive

Enzymes do

  • Lower activation energy
  • Speed up forward and reverse reactions
  • Help the reaction reach equilibrium faster
  • Come out unchanged and get reused

Enzymes do not

  • Change ΔG (the energy difference between reactants and products)
  • Change where the equilibrium lies
  • Make an endergonic reaction happen without an energy source
  • Get used up in the reaction

How the active site lowers Eₐ

  • Orientation: holds two substrates in exactly the right position to react.
  • Strain: induced fit bends the substrate, stressing the bonds that will break.
  • Microenvironment: R groups create conditions (for example, acidic) that the reaction favors.
  • Direct participation: R groups briefly bond with the substrate during the reaction.

Measuring reaction rate

Data skill

Product formed by an enzyme over time. The dashed line is the initial rate: the slope of the first, nearly straight part of the curve.

Rate = change in amount ÷ change in time. Measure either product appearing or substrate disappearing. From this graph, initial rate ≈ (20 µmol − 0) ÷ (2 min − 0) = 10 µmol/min.

The curve flattens because the substrate runs out, not because the enzyme is used up. Add more substrate and production starts again.

Exam move

"Calculate the rate" means pick two points on the linear part, show the subtraction, and include units (µmol/min, mL O₂/min). Missing units can cost the point.

Topic 3.3

Environmental Impacts on Enzyme Function

Temperature, pH, salt, substrate amount and other molecules all change how fast an enzyme works. Most of them do it by changing shape.

You should be able to
  • Explain how changes in conditions affect enzyme activity.
  • Explain how inhibitors and other molecules regulate enzymes.

Enzyme bench

RELATIVE RATE
100%
At optimum

Temperature

Warming increases rate at first: molecules move faster and collide more often. Past the optimum, heat breaks the hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold the tertiary structure. The enzyme denatures, the active site loses its shape, and rate drops sharply. Cold slows enzymes but usually doesn't denature them.

pH

Each enzyme has an optimal pH. Extra or missing H⁺ changes the charges on R groups, which disrupts ionic and hydrogen bonds. Pepsin works near pH 2 in the stomach; trypsin near pH 8 in the small intestine.

Substrate concentration

More substrate means more collisions, so rate rises, until every active site is busy. That is saturation; the rate levels off at its maximum (Vmax). To go faster, you would need more enzyme.

Enzyme concentration and salt

With plenty of substrate, rate rises in proportion to enzyme concentration. High salt concentration interferes with ionic bonds in the protein and can also change its shape.

Inhibitors

An inhibitor is a molecule that reduces enzyme activity. Cells use inhibitors to regulate pathways; many drugs and poisons are inhibitors too.

Irreversible inhibitors bond covalently and permanently disable the enzyme (nerve gases, and penicillin acting on a bacterial enzyme). Most cell regulators are reversible.

Feedback inhibition

In a metabolic pathway, the end product often acts as an allosteric inhibitor of the first enzyme. When enough product builds up, the pathway shuts itself off, so the cell doesn't waste energy and raw materials.

Cofactors

Inorganic helpers an enzyme needs to work, often metal ions like Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺ or Fe²⁺.

Coenzymes

Organic cofactors, many made from vitamins. NAD⁺ (from niacin, vitamin B₃) carries electrons for dozens of enzymes.

Exam move

For a high-temperature result, say "denatured," then explain it: bonds holding the tertiary structure break → active site changes shape → substrate can't bind. For a low-temperature result, say "fewer collisions," not "denatured."

Topic 3.4

Cellular Energy

Life runs on a budget. Cells pay for building and moving with ATP, and they earn it by breaking down food.

You should be able to
  • Describe the role of energy in living organisms.
  • Explain how cells couple energy-releasing and energy-using reactions.

The laws that set the rules

1st law of thermodynamics

Energy can't be created or destroyed, only converted. Light becomes chemical energy in glucose; glucose energy becomes ATP and heat.

2nd law of thermodynamics

Every energy transfer increases entropy (disorder), and some energy is lost as heat. To stay organized, cells need a constant input of energy. If the input stops, order breaks down and the organism dies.

Free energy: which reactions go on their own

ΔG is the change in free energy, the energy available to do work. Its sign tells you whether a reaction releases or requires energy.

ExergonicEndergonic
ΔGNegative (< 0)Positive (> 0)
EnergyReleasedAbsorbed

ATP: the cell's energy currency

Diagram

ATP = adenine + ribose + three phosphate groups. The three negatively charged phosphates repel each other, so removing the last one releases energy.

ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pᵢ   ΔG ≈ −7.3 kcal/mol (−30.5 kJ/mol)

Cells recycle ATP constantly. Respiration adds the phosphate back to ADP; work spends it again. A working muscle cell recycles its whole ATP supply in about a minute.

Energy coupling

Cells drive endergonic reactions by pairing them with an exergonic one, usually ATP hydrolysis. ATP transfers its phosphate to a reactant (phosphorylation). The phosphorylated molecule is less stable and more reactive, so the next step can go. As long as the total ΔG is negative, the coupled reaction runs.

Worked example: making glutamineΔG (kcal/mol)
Glutamic acid + NH₃ → glutamine (on its own)+3.4
ATP → ADP + Pᵢ−7.3
Coupled total−3.9

ATP also powers active transport (the Na⁺/K⁺ pump) and mechanical work (motor proteins moving along the cytoskeleton).

Metabolic pathways

Cells rarely do a big chemical change in one step. A metabolic pathway is a series of steps, each catalyzed by its own enzyme, where the product of one step is the substrate of the next. Releasing energy in small steps lets the cell capture it instead of losing it all as heat.

Because each step needs its enzyme, losing one enzyme (through a mutation, say) stops the pathway at that point: the substrate before the block builds up, and nothing after it gets made.

Exam move

"Why do organisms need a constant energy supply?" Answer with the 2nd law: energy transfers are never 100% efficient, some is always lost as heat, and maintaining order requires replacing it.

Topic 3.5 · Overview

Photosynthesis

Light energy becomes chemical energy in two linked stages. The light reactions make ATP and NADPH; the Calvin cycle spends them to build sugar from CO₂.

You should be able to
  • Describe the photosynthetic processes that allow organisms to capture and store energy.
  • Explain how cells capture energy from light and transfer it to biological molecules.
6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

Two stages, one chloroplast

Diagram

The light reactions hand ATP and NADPH to the Calvin cycle; the Calvin cycle hands back ADP, Pᵢ and NADP⁺. Stop either stage and the other soon stops too.

Where it happens

Thylakoid membrane

Holds the pigments, photosystems, electron transport chain and ATP synthase. The light reactions happen here.

Thylakoid lumen

The space inside each thylakoid. H⁺ builds up here, making it acidic (about pH 5).

Stroma

The fluid around the thylakoids (about pH 8). The Calvin cycle runs here. Stacks of thylakoids are called grana.

Where it came from

Photosynthesis first evolved in prokaryotes. Cyanobacteria released the O₂ that built up in Earth's early atmosphere, which later made aerobic respiration possible. Chloroplasts descend from a cyanobacterium engulfed by an early eukaryote (endosymbiosis). The evidence: chloroplasts have a double membrane, their own circular DNA, and their own 70S ribosomes, and they divide by a process like binary fission.

Topic 3.5 · Stage 1

Light-Dependent Reactions

Light pushes electrons from water uphill to NADP⁺. On the way, their energy pumps H⁺ into the thylakoid, and the H⁺ gradient makes ATP.

Inputs

Light, H₂O, NADP⁺, ADP + Pᵢ

Outputs

O₂ (released as a by-product), ATP and NADPH (sent to the Calvin cycle)

Thylakoid membrane

Click each part

The sequence

  1. Pigments in photosystem II absorb light and pass the energy to the reaction-center chlorophyll a (P680), which loses an excited electron to the primary acceptor.
  2. PSII replaces that electron by splitting water: 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂. The H⁺ stays in the lumen; the O₂ leaves the leaf.
  3. Electrons travel through plastoquinone to the cytochrome complex, which uses their energy to pump H⁺ from the stroma into the lumen.
  4. Plastocyanin carries electrons to photosystem I (P700), where light re-energizes them.
  5. Ferredoxin passes them to NADP⁺ reductase, which makes NADPH on the stroma side.
  6. H⁺ flows from the lumen back to the stroma through ATP synthase, which makes ATP (photophosphorylation by chemiosmosis).

Same trick as mitochondria

Both organelles use an ETC to pump H⁺ and ATP synthase to make ATP. In chloroplasts, H⁺ is pumped into the thylakoid lumen and ATP forms in the stroma. In mitochondria, H⁺ is pumped into the intermembrane space and ATP forms in the matrix.

Proof the gradient alone makes ATP

Jagendorf soaked isolated thylakoids in pH 4 buffer in the dark, then moved them to pH 8 with ADP and Pᵢ. They made ATP with no light at all: the H⁺ gradient was enough.

Exam move

The light reactions make no sugar and use no CO₂. If a question asks where O₂ comes from, answer "photolysis of water at photosystem II," never "from CO₂."

Topic 3.5 · Stage 2

The Calvin Cycle

In the stroma, CO₂ from the air is attached to an existing sugar, then reduced with ATP and NADPH into G3P, the 3-carbon sugar plants build everything else from.

Inputs

CO₂, ATP, NADPH (and RuBP, which the cycle regenerates)

Outputs

G3P (to make glucose, sucrose, starch, cellulose, amino acids), plus ADP, Pᵢ and NADP⁺ returned to the light reactions

Calvin cycle, three turns

Diagram
To make…CO₂ fixedATP usedNADPH used
1 G3P (3 turns)396
1 glucose (6 turns)61812

The three phases

  1. Carbon fixation. Rubisco attaches each CO₂ to RuBP (5C). The unstable 6C product splits into two molecules of 3-PGA (3C). Rubisco is slow, so leaves pack in huge amounts of it; it may be the most abundant protein on Earth.
  2. Reduction. Each 3-PGA gets a phosphate from ATP and then electrons from NADPH, becoming G3P. This is where the energy from the light reactions is stored in sugar.
  3. Regeneration. Five of every six G3P are rearranged, using more ATP, to rebuild three RuBP so the cycle can keep fixing CO₂. One G3P leaves the cycle as profit.

Classic data question

Beyond the basics: C₄ and CAM plants

On hot, dry days, leaves close their stomata to save water, so CO₂ inside the leaf runs low and rubisco works poorly. C₄ plants (corn, sugarcane) first fix CO₂ into a 4-carbon compound in one cell type and release it next to rubisco in another, keeping CO₂ concentrated. CAM plants (cacti, pineapple) open stomata only at night, store CO₂ as acids, and run the Calvin cycle by day with stomata closed. Both are adaptations that improve fitness in hot, dry environments.

Exam move

"Light-independent" does not mean "happens in the dark." The Calvin cycle needs a steady supply of ATP and NADPH, so in practice it runs only while the light reactions do.

How much light of each wavelength a single pigment absorbs, measured with a spectrophotometer.

Action spectrum

How fast photosynthesis runs at each wavelength. It matches the combined absorption of all the pigments, which shows that accessory pigments pass energy to chlorophyll a.

Engelmann's experiment (1883): he shone a spectrum of light across a strand of algae in water with aerobic bacteria. The bacteria crowded around the parts of the alga lit by red and blue light. Those were the places releasing the most O₂, so they were photosynthesizing fastest.

Topic 3.6 · Overview

Cellular Respiration

Glucose is taken apart step by step. Its electrons are handed to NADH and FADH₂, and their energy ends up in about 30 ATP.

You should be able to
  • Describe the processes cells use to harvest energy from organic molecules.
  • Explain how cells obtain energy from biological macromolecules to power cellular processes.
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy (ATP + heat)

Follow the electrons

Respiration is a series of redox reactions. Glucose is oxidized (loses electrons) and O₂ is reduced (gains them, becoming water). Remember OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain. Along the way, the electron carriers NAD⁺ and FAD pick up electrons and become NADH and FADH₂. Releasing the energy in many small steps lets the cell capture it as ATP instead of losing it all as heat, the way burning sugar would.

Not just glucose

Other food molecules feed into the same pathway at different points. Fats are broken into glycerol (enters glycolysis) and fatty acids (chopped into acetyl-CoA, which enters the Krebs cycle); a gram of fat yields more than twice the ATP of a gram of carbohydrate. Proteins are broken into amino acids, whose amino groups are removed and whose carbon skeletons enter glycolysis or the Krebs cycle.

Topic 3.6 · Stage 1

Glycolysis

"Sugar splitting." Ten enzyme steps in the cytosol break one glucose into two pyruvate, with a small ATP profit and no oxygen required.

Location: cytosolO₂: not required

Inputs

1 glucose (6C), 2 ATP, 2 NAD⁺, 4 ADP + 4 Pᵢ

Outputs

2 pyruvate (3C each), 4 ATP (net 2), 2 NADH, 2 H₂O

Energy-investment phase

The cell spends 2 ATP to phosphorylate glucose. The extra phosphates make the sugar less stable and trap it inside the cell. The 6-carbon sugar then splits into two 3-carbon G3P molecules.

Energy-payoff phase

Each G3P is oxidized: NAD⁺ picks up electrons to become NADH. Enzymes then move phosphates directly onto ADP, making 4 ATP in total. Net: 4 − 2 = 2 ATP.

Glycolysis ledger (per glucose)ATPNADH

Substrate-level phosphorylation

Glycolysis makes ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation: an enzyme takes a phosphate from a phosphorylated substrate and hands it straight to ADP. No membrane or H⁺ gradient is involved, which is why glycolysis works in the cytosol and in cells without mitochondria, such as red blood cells.

Regulation: phosphofructokinase (PFK)

Feedback

PFK catalyzes an early, committed step of glycolysis and is an allosteric enzyme. Its activity tracks the cell's energy needs.

Evidence that glycolysis is ancient

Nearly every living organism, from bacteria to humans, uses the same glycolysis enzymes. It runs in the cytosol without membrane-bound organelles or O₂, which fits an origin in early prokaryotes before oxygen built up in the atmosphere. Shared pathways like this are evidence of common ancestry.

Exam move

Glycolysis needs a steady supply of NAD⁺. If NADH can't be turned back into NAD⁺ (no O₂ and no fermentation), glycolysis stops. This is the key to every fermentation question.

Topic 3.6 · Stage 2

Pyruvate Oxidation

The link between glycolysis and the Krebs cycle. Each pyruvate loses a carbon as CO₂ and becomes acetyl-CoA.

Location: mitochondrial matrixO₂: needed indirectly

What happens to each pyruvate

  1. Pyruvate is actively transported from the cytosol into the mitochondrial matrix (in prokaryotes it stays in the cytosol).
  2. Its carboxyl group is removed and released as CO₂. This is the first CO₂ released in respiration.
  3. The remaining 2-carbon fragment is oxidized; its electrons reduce NAD⁺ to NADH.
  4. Coenzyme A is attached, forming acetyl-CoA, which is ready to enter the Krebs cycle.

One enzyme complex, pyruvate dehydrogenase, does all of these chemical steps.

Why it needs O₂ indirectly

This step uses no O₂ itself, but it needs NAD⁺. NAD⁺ is regenerated only when the ETC passes NADH's electrons to O₂. No O₂ → NADH piles up → pyruvate oxidation stops.

When the link breaks

If pyruvate dehydrogenase is defective, pyruvate can't enter the Krebs cycle. It builds up and is converted to lactate, so patients develop lactic acid buildup and tissues with high energy demands (brain, muscle) suffer most.

Topic 3.6 · Stage 3

The Krebs Cycle

Also called the citric acid cycle. It finishes oxidizing the carbons from glucose and loads their electrons onto NADH and FADH₂.

Location: mitochondrial matrixO₂: needed indirectly

One turn of the cycle

Diagram

Simplified: eight steps shown as four. Acetyl-CoA (2C) joins oxaloacetate (4C) to make citrate (6C). Two carbons leave as CO₂ and oxaloacetate is rebuilt, so the cycle can turn again.

Why it matters

After the Krebs cycle, glucose is completely oxidized to CO₂. Only 4 ATP have been made so far (2 from glycolysis, 2 here). Most of the energy is now held as high-energy electrons in 10 NADH and 2 FADH₂, which go to the electron transport chain.

Inhibitor example: malonate

Malonate looks like succinate and competitively inhibits succinate dehydrogenase. Add it to mitochondria and succinate builds up, FADH₂ production drops, and the cycle slows. Intermediates before a blocked step accumulate; those after it run low.

Not only for energy

Krebs intermediates are also raw materials. Cells draw off α-ketoglutarate and oxaloacetate to build amino acids, and citrate to build fatty acids. The cycle connects energy release to biosynthesis.

Exam move

The Krebs cycle doesn't use O₂, yet it stops within seconds without it. Explain this through NAD⁺ and FAD: without O₂ the ETC can't reoxidize NADH and FADH₂, so the cycle runs out of electron acceptors.

Topic 3.6 · Stage 4

Electron Transport & Chemiosmosis

NADH and FADH₂ unload their electrons. The electrons' energy pumps H⁺, and the H⁺ flowing back through ATP synthase makes about 90% of the cell's ATP.

Location: inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae)O₂: required as the final electron acceptor

Inner mitochondrial membrane

Click each part

Two linked processes

  1. Electron transport. Electrons pass from carrier to carrier, each more electronegative than the last, releasing energy in small steps. Complexes I, III and IV use that energy to pump H⁺ from the matrix into the intermembrane space. O₂ takes the electrons at the end and forms water.
  2. Chemiosmosis. The H⁺ gradient (more H⁺ and lower pH in the intermembrane space) is stored energy. H⁺ can cross back only through ATP synthase, whose spinning rotor joins ADP and Pᵢ into ATP. Together these steps are oxidative phosphorylation.

What if…? Mitochondria under attack

FRQ favorite

Pick a condition and predict the effects before you read them.

Brown fat and thermogenesis

Brown fat cells are packed with mitochondria containing thermogenin, an uncoupling protein that lets H⁺ leak back into the matrix. The ETC runs flat out but the energy becomes heat instead of ATP. Newborns and hibernating mammals use this to stay warm.

In prokaryotes

Bacteria have no mitochondria. Their ETC and ATP synthase sit in the plasma membrane, and they pump H⁺ out of the cell. Some use other final electron acceptors, such as nitrate or sulfate (anaerobic respiration).

Exam move

The ETC itself makes no ATP. Its job is to build the H⁺ gradient; ATP synthase makes the ATP. For any inhibitor, predict three things in order: electron flow, H⁺ gradient, ATP production (then O₂ use).

Topic 3.6 · No oxygen

Fermentation

Without O₂, the ETC stops and NAD⁺ runs out. Fermentation recycles NADH back to NAD⁺ so glycolysis can keep making its 2 ATP.

Location: cytosolO₂: not used

Why fermentation exists

Glycolysis needs NAD⁺ to accept electrons. Normally the ETC regenerates NAD⁺ by passing NADH's electrons to O₂. With no O₂, NADH builds up and glycolysis would stop. Fermentation hands NADH's electrons to pyruvate (or a molecule made from it) instead, regenerating NAD⁺. The fermentation step itself makes no ATP; all the ATP comes from glycolysis.

Alcohol fermentation

pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO₂
acetaldehyde + NADH → ethanol + NAD⁺

Yeast and some bacteria. The CO₂ makes bread rise and beer bubble.

Lactic acid fermentation

pyruvate + NADH → lactate + NAD⁺

Muscle cells during intense exercise, and bacteria that make yogurt and cheese. No CO₂ is released.

The Pasteur effect

Because fermentation gets about 15 times less ATP per glucose, yeast switched from aerobic to anaerobic conditions burns through glucose much faster to meet the same energy needs.

Who can live without O₂

Obligate anaerobes are poisoned by O₂. Facultative anaerobes (yeast, E. coli) respire when O₂ is present and ferment when it isn't. Obligate aerobes, like us, need O₂, though our muscle cells can ferment for short bursts.

Exam move

"What is the purpose of fermentation?" The scoring answer is to regenerate NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue producing ATP. Saying "to make ATP" alone doesn't earn the point.

Topic 3.7

Fitness

Variety at the molecular level gives cells more options, and more options help organisms survive and reproduce when conditions change.

You should be able to
  • Explain how variation in the number and types of molecules in cells affects an organism's ability to survive and reproduce in different environments.

An organism whose cells have several versions of a molecule can work across a wider range of conditions. In a population, that variation means some individuals are more likely to survive an environmental change. The AP exam uses a handful of standard examples. Learn what each one is and why the variety helps.

Try it

The absorption spectrum on the Pigments & rate tab lets you switch pigments off one at a time and see how much of the visible spectrum a plant would lose. That is molecular variation improving fitness.

Chlorophylls and accessory pigments

Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and carotenoids absorb different wavelengths. Together they capture more of the light spectrum than any one pigment, so the plant can photosynthesize in more light conditions (shade, different depths of water).

Membrane phospholipids

Cells adjust their mix of fatty acids. More unsaturated tails (with kinks) keep membranes fluid in the cold; more saturated tails keep them from getting too fluid in the heat.

Hemoglobin types

Fetal hemoglobin binds O₂ more tightly than adult hemoglobin, so a fetus can pull oxygen from the mother's blood across the placenta. Some high-altitude animals have hemoglobin with higher O₂ affinity too.

MHC proteins

Many versions of MHC proteins in a population let individuals present a wide range of pathogen fragments to the immune system, so some individuals survive any given disease.

Antibody diversity

Shuffling of antibody gene segments gives each person millions of different antibodies, so the immune system can recognize almost any antigen.

Enzyme versions

Different forms of an enzyme can have different temperature or pH optima. Taq polymerase from hot-spring bacteria works near 72 °C, where most enzymes would denature. Try it on the 3.3 enzyme bench.

Exam move

Use the pattern: more variety of molecule X → cell can function across a wider range of condition Y → higher chance of survival and reproduction. Name the molecule, the condition, and the fitness payoff.

Labs & experimental design

The Three Unit 3 Labs

FRQs often describe one of these setups and ask you to identify variables, predict a result, or justify a control.

Enzyme activity (peroxidase or catalase)

3.2–3.3

Reaction: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. Peroxidase from turnips is often paired with guaiacol, which turns brown as the reaction proceeds; catalase from yeast or liver gives off visible O₂ bubbles.

INDEPENDENT VARIABLE

Temperature, pH, enzyme concentration or substrate concentration (one at a time)

DEPENDENT VARIABLE

Rate: color change per minute (absorbance) or O₂ produced per minute

CONTROLS

Tube with boiled (denatured) enzyme or no enzyme; same volumes, time and other conditions in every tube

Photosynthesis (floating leaf disks)

3.5

Spinach leaf disks have air pulled out of them with a syringe and sink in a sodium bicarbonate solution (the bicarbonate supplies CO₂). Under light, photosynthesis produces O₂, which collects in the leaf's air spaces until the disks float.

INDEPENDENT VARIABLE

Light intensity, light color (wavelength), or CO₂ (bicarbonate) concentration

DEPENDENT VARIABLE

Time for 50% of the disks to float (ET₅₀). Shorter time = faster rate

CONTROLS

Disks in water with no bicarbonate (no CO₂), and disks kept in the dark

Cellular respiration (respirometer)

3.6

Germinating peas sit in a sealed vial with KOH, which absorbs the CO₂ they release. As peas consume O₂, gas volume in the vial drops and water moves into the pipette. The water movement measures O₂ consumed.

INDEPENDENT VARIABLE

Germinating vs. dormant seeds; temperature

DEPENDENT VARIABLE

mL of O₂ consumed per minute

CONTROLS

Glass beads (no respiration) correct for temperature and pressure changes; equal volume in every vial

Germinating seeds respire faster than dormant ones because they're growing and need more ATP. Raising the temperature (below the enzyme optimum) also raises the rate.

Experimental design checklist

  • Hypothesis: a testable "if… then… because" statement.
  • Independent variable: what you deliberately change.
  • Dependent variable: what you measure.
  • Controlled variables: everything kept the same. Name specific ones (volume, temperature, time), not "everything else."
  • Negative control: a setup where you expect no effect (boiled enzyme, glass beads).
  • Replication: several trials to reduce the effect of random error.
  • Error bars: if 95% confidence intervals (±2 SE) of two groups don't overlap, the difference is likely significant.

Free-response practice

Write your answer first, then open the model answer and compare.

FRQ 1: Enzyme data (3.3)

A student measures the rate of a stomach enzyme at pH 1, 2, 4, 6 and 8 at 37 °C. Rates (µmol/min): 6, 11, 5, 1, 0.

  1. Identify the independent and dependent variables.
  2. Identify the optimal pH and explain why activity is near zero at pH 8.
  3. Predict what happens if the enzyme at pH 8 is returned to pH 2, and justify your prediction.
Model answer

(a) IV: pH. DV: reaction rate (µmol product/min).

(b) Optimum ≈ pH 2. At pH 8 the low H⁺ concentration changes the charges on R groups, disrupting the ionic and hydrogen bonds that maintain the enzyme's tertiary structure. The active site changes shape, so substrate can no longer bind.

(c) Activity likely stays low (or recovers only partly), because denaturation is often irreversible: once the enzyme unfolds it may not refold into its functional shape. (Credit also goes to "activity returns" if justified by reversible shape change; the justification is what earns the point.)

FRQ 2: Blocking ATP synthase (3.6)

Oligomycin is a drug that blocks the H⁺ channel of ATP synthase. Predict the effect of oligomycin on (a) the H⁺ concentration in the intermembrane space, (b) ATP production, and (c) the rate of O₂ consumption. Justify each prediction.

Model answer

(a) Increases. The ETC keeps pumping H⁺ into the intermembrane space, but H⁺ can't flow back through ATP synthase.

(b) Decreases sharply. Oxidative phosphorylation needs H⁺ flowing through ATP synthase; only the small amount from glycolysis and the Krebs cycle (substrate-level) remains.

(c) Decreases. As the gradient gets steeper, pumping more H⁺ against it becomes too hard, so the ETC slows. Fewer electrons reach O₂, so less O₂ is consumed.

FRQ 3: Design a leaf-disk experiment (3.5)

Design an experiment using the floating leaf disk method to test how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis. Include a prediction and justify it.

Model answer

Set up 4 cups of 0.2% sodium bicarbonate with 10 vacuum-infiltrated spinach disks each. Place a lamp at 10, 20, 40 and 80 cm (IV: light intensity). Keep bicarbonate concentration, solution volume, temperature, disk size and leaf source the same. Record the number of floating disks every minute and calculate the time for 50% to float (DV). Include a cup kept in the dark as a negative control. Run 3 trials of each.

Prediction: disks closest to the light float fastest. More light energy → more electrons excited in the photosystems → faster light reactions → more O₂ produced from splitting water → O₂ fills the air spaces sooner. At very high intensity the rate levels off because another factor (CO₂ or enzymes like rubisco) becomes limiting.
